# Could Lean help the FPGA design agent? An assessment and one small proof (brief 124)

Written 2026-11-20 after the Latent Space article "The War Against Slop and an Emerging Weapon to Fight it: Formal Verification" (Richard MacManus, 2026-11-20,
https://latent.space/p/formal-verification/). Evidence for the experiment: [formal/lean](../formal/lean/README.md) and [results/124_lean](../results/124_lean/README.md).
Literature was searched on 2026-11-20 with a US-only web search and a few page fetches; anything not read on the source's own page is marked UNCONFIRMED.

## Short answer

Yes, in one narrow place: proving, for all widths and all lengths, that a datapath **algorithm** or **arithmetic identity** is right, and recording that proof as evidence next to the simulation and SymbiYosys results.
No, for control logic, handshakes, clock crossings and liveness, where model checking stays the better tool. The experiment proved the checksum equations of brief 119's TCP fast path for all inputs, with a model writing
the proofs and Lean's kernel checking them. The model's first try did not compile; it took four rounds of checker feedback. The approach is **not new**: three arXiv papers from May to July 2026 already combine LLM agents, Lean 4 and RTL (see the survey). What may be new is the combination described under "What can honestly be claimed".

## 1. Where Lean fits, honestly

| | SymbiYosys and SMT (what the project uses) | Lean 4 |
|---|---|---|
| Strength | push-button; finite-state; k-induction and BMC; returns a counterexample trace | proofs for all widths, lengths and parameters; algebraic correctness of an algorithm; the specification is a precise, checkable object |
| Stops at | unbounded parameters (a proof per width, as in brief 115's gray-code proof); arithmetic identities over all widths; liveness induction will not close (115's rate-matcher progress is only bounded); algorithm-level correctness | control-heavy state machines and anything where the counterexample is the useful output; cost per proof is higher |
| Who writes the proof | the tool, after a human writes properties and sometimes helper invariants | a human or a model; the kernel checks it, so a model's mistakes cannot be accepted |
| Gap to the RTL | small: it reads the RTL (through Yosys) | large: the Lean model is a separate object; the link is a transcription (manual, or generated by tools such as Rtl2lean) |
| Trust base | the SMT solver and Yosys | Lean's small kernel; plus the compiler for `bv_decide` lemmas (see below) |

Things the experiment taught:

- **`bv_decide` is SymbiYosys's cousin inside Lean.** It bit-blasts a bit-vector goal to SAT and checks the certificate. It proved the small fixed-width lemmas. It is limited to fixed widths, and in Lean 4.36 each use adds an axiom
  `<lemma>._native.bv_decide.ax_*`: the certificate is checked by compiled code, so the Lean compiler joins the trusted base for that lemma (the Lean reference documentation and the 4.12.0 release notes describe the earlier `Lean.ofReduceBool` form; they disagree on the trust point, so check the version in use). We therefore report two tiers: **kernel only** and **uses `bv_decide`**.
- **What Lean added here:** the statement over lists of any length (the sum of any number of 16-bit words equals the natural-number sum reduced mod 65535) and the RFC 1624 incremental update for a header of any length. A model checker proves a given length. Lean also found, as a theorem with a side condition, the all-zero-header corner where RFC 1624's equation gives 0x0000 instead of 0xFFFF (`incUpdate_zero_corner`).
- **What Lean did not add:** the RTL-to-model link. It is a manual transcription, cross-checked on 300 random frames against brief 119's Python reference (600 `#guard` lines). The proofs cannot see a transcription error.
- **What costs more:** the model's first try took 13.6 minutes of model time and 27 errors; later rounds took about a minute each. A human still has to read the statements and the transcription; that reading is the specification and the link.

## 3. The experiment

Source: [formal/lean/README.md](../formal/lean/README.md). The statements (all proved) are RTL equations of `tcp_fast.sv` against recomputation of the Internet checksum, the receiver identity, a list-of-any-length model of the sum, and RFC 1624's update. The loop was: the executor wrote `Spec.lean` with every proof `sorry`; Sonnet 5.5 through the 108 table's `claude_code` endpoint returned a proved file; `lake build` ran; errors went back; a script checked that no definition or statement changed.

- First try: 27 errors, did not build (815.7 s of model time). After four rounds of feedback (57.5, 61.8, 50.6 and 59.9 s): builds, no `sorry`. The statements were unchanged (`tools/statements.py`).
- Result: 27 theorems (6 state the specification, 21 are helper lemmas the model wrote) in about 450 lines. Of the six, three are kernel-only (`ocSum_toNat`, `incUpdate_correct`, `incUpdate_zero_corner`) and three (`rtl_ip_correct`, `rtl_ip_receiver`, `rtl_tcp_correct`) depend on three small `bv_decide` lemmas; of the helpers 16 are kernel-only and 5 depend on `bv_decide` (`results/124_lean/lean_evidence.json`).
- The model could not remove `bv_decide` in six further rounds (no Lean error text in half of them, an invented lemma name in the rest). A tool-using agent was not tried.
- What a human must still check: the **statements** (they are the specification; the executor wrote them, the owner has not approved them) and the **RTL transcription** (manual, cross-checked on 300 frames).

## 4. What can honestly be claimed (the owner's question about "new ground")

**Not claimable:** that ITP on hardware is new (ACL2 at AMD, Kami, Silver predate Lean 4); that LLM agents with Lean on RTL are new (Rtl2lean, CircuitProver and CktFormalizer, May to July 2026, do this and are peer work to be cited); that this experiment is a first. It is one small proof about one datapath piece.

**Candidate novelty, to be stated as a combination, as far as the search showed:** an LLM agent that designs the RTL for a real FPGA, a human who writes and approves the theorem statements (the specification), a script that checks that a proved file leaves every definition and statement unchanged, the Lean kernel as the checker with an axiom audit that
separates kernel-only proofs from SAT-certificate ones, the RTL link cross-checked against an independent reference, and the proof recorded as computed evidence in the same traceability matrix as simulation and SymbiYosys results. The search found no work that records Lean proofs in a requirements traceability matrix for an FPGA flow; a shallow US-only search does not show that none exists. In this repo the last part is a **proposal** (below), not yet in `flow/traceability.py`.

Defensible interview phrasing: "ITP on hardware has a long history and LLM agents writing Lean proofs about RTL are an active research area; I ran a small, honest instance on my own design: the specification was fixed and approved by a human, a model wrote the proofs with the checker's feedback, Lean's kernel checked them, and I kept the trust tiers explicit. I did not invent the approach; what I added was putting it in the evidence matrix next to the SymbiYosys proofs."

## 5. Recommendation

1. **Add a "proved for all widths" tier** for datapath algorithms and arithmetic identities where per-width SymbiYosys proofs do not cover the claim: checksums, gray-code one-bit steps, CRC and parity identities, modular and fixed-point arithmetic (NTT butterflies, rounding), the algorithm layer of a divider or a sorter. One theorem per requirement clause.
2. **Use it as a spec-checking role.** Writing a statement forces the requirement into a precise form; reading a statement is cheaper for a human than reading a proof. This fits the project's "the human specifies and judges; the agent implements".
3. **Keep SymbiYosys for** control logic, handshakes, FIFOs, clock-crossing protocols, bounded progress, and anything whose counterexample matters.
4. **Costs and risks.** A separate model of the RTL (transcription risk, mitigated by a cross-check and, later, by generation from RTL as Rtl2lean does); a 3 GB toolchain; proof-writing time (about 17 minutes of model time here, 13.6 of them for the first, failed attempt; a human must still read the statements); a trust base that needs two tiers; a model can write a theorem that is true but too weak, so statements need human approval and a frozen-statement check.
5. **Next step if adopted (a brief of its own):** the gray-code one-bit-step lemma for all widths (complements brief 115), then a Rtl2lean-style generated link for one small module, then the matrix method below. Do not add Lean to CI until the toolchain is cached.

## 6. Proposed `formal_lean` method for the traceability matrix (not implemented; flow/traceability.py untouched)

- `spec.yaml` requirement `proof:` list gains `formal_lean`.
- Tag: a `req: REQ-ID` line inside the theorem's doc comment (the experiment uses `B119-CKSUM-IP`, `-TCP`, `-INC`, `-MODEL`; they are placeholders, 119's spec has no such ids yet).
- Artefact kind `lean_theorem`, read from `lean_evidence.json` (schema in `formal/lean/tools/lean_evidence.py`): theorem, line, req tags, statement SHA-256, axioms, status computed from `lake build` and `#print axioms`: `proved` (kernel only), `proved_bv_decide`, `failed`, `unsound`.
- Gates: `requirements_verified` passes the method on `proved` or `proved_bv_decide` and fails on `failed` or `unsound`; a new gate `lean_statements_unchanged` compares each statement's hash with the hash recorded in the approved spec; `changed_requirements_reverified` already forces a re-check when a requirement's text changes.
- The review index shows the tier next to the result, so a reader sees which proofs rest on the compiler.
