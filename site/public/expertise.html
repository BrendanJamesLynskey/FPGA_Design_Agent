Areas of expertise

FPGA design draws on many specialisms, from clock crossings to memory controllers. This page takes them one at a time and says, for each, what the agent knows, what its flow checks on every run, what has been measured and what is not yet shown.

Each area is a drawer. It opens with a few plain sentences, then the measurements with a link to the file each comes from, then the gaps. The lists behind it (the skills the agent can load, the checks in the flow, and where AMD's advice differs between device families) sit in a Details section inside the drawer, closed until you want them.

Constraints and timing closureWriting clocks, I/O delays and exceptions that are right, and reading timing reports to close the paths that fail.24 skills, 7 flow checks Checked by the flow

Constraints tell Vivado what the clocks are, when signals arrive at the pins and which paths need no timing; timing closure is the work of making every remaining path fast enough. The agent writes the constraints from its skills, and a gate after routing fails any run that misses setup, hold or pulse-width timing or leaves a path unconstrained. A separate checker looks for risky lines, such as a false path with no stated reason.

Measured. On 10 small designs with one timing-exception mistake each, Vivado itself said something about the exception in 5; the flow's checks flagged 10. They passed 4 of 4 correct designs. source On 13 small designs with one I/O-delay or virtual-clock mistake each, Vivado itself said something in 2; the flow's gates flagged 13. source On a two-clock probe design, setup slack on the crossing was -1.364 ns without AMD's macro exceptions and 8.891 ns with them (Vivado 2026.1). source

In agent runs. Of the development runs whose saved designs were rebuilt on Vivado 2026.1, 6 reached routing; setup slack was positive in 6. source In 3 agent runs of the slow-controller drill (one clock, a one-in-eight enable, an eight-clock multicycle), the constraint grader passed 2; the other run put a register that a write sets on any clock into the eight-clock group, which only the grader's simulation check caught. source In 3 agent runs of the ADC-capture drill (input delays from a data sheet, the oscillator's jitter, a margin for implementation only), the delays were right in 3, free of made-up delays in 3 and carried the jitter in 3; the implementation-only margin was right in 0. source

Not yet shown. I/O delay constraints, virtual clocks and clock uncertainty are written from the skills; no committed run scores them against a data sheet. The flow's multicycle check looks at each register's clock-enable logic, not at whether the register really changes only on the enable; the drill grader adds that check in simulation, the flow does not yet. Only one exception drill is built (the slow controller); the two-cycle datapath, the quasi-static register and the seeded wrong false path are not. Exceptions and I/O delays are now shown in agent runs, but one exception run wrote a wrong multicycle that the flow passed, and no I/O run kept its implementation margin out of sign-off the way asked; virtual clocks have no agent runs yet.

Clock-domain and reset-domain crossingSynchronisers, multi-bit crossings, asynchronous FIFOs, reset bridges and the checks that find a crossing report_cdc passes but that is still wrong.15 skills, 5 flow checks Checked by the flow

Signals that pass between unrelated clocks need synchronisers, and a mistake there can pass every simulation and still fail on the bench. The agent knows the standard circuits for single bits, buses, FIFOs and resets, and its flow checks each crossing three ways: Vivado's crossing report, a structural check of every flip-flop fed from another clock, and a simulation that makes synchronisers resolve late.

Measured. A clock-uncertainty margin is not crossing margin. On Artix-7 it left a crossing's slack unchanged (5.34 and 5.34 ns) and failed hold elsewhere (worst -0.692 ns); on MAX10 it turned a passing path (0.76 ns) into a failing one (-0.04 ns); in Quartus a clock group removed the skew check (96 paths with bounds, 0 with the group). source On 10 ported broken crossings, report_cdc alone flagged 5; the flow's gate flagged 10. It passed 12 of 12 correct variants. source On the 12 mutants of the soak rig, its simulation with injection flagged 9. source

Not yet shown. No agent run has used the stronger crossing gate yet. A reset cleared on one side only, in an asynchronous FIFO or a handshake, gets past the gate; only the rig's simulation catches it. The gate's simulation works cycle by cycle, so it finds lost pulses and bits crossed separately but not route skew, glitches or true metastability. The bus-skew gate has no failing case from our own designs: neither tested part could be routed past a realistic bound; its negative case is a recorded over-tight bound.

ClockingChoosing an MMCM or PLL setting, clock buffers, dynamic phase and glitch-free switching, and using the Clocking Wizard as a checker.12 skills, 6 flow checks Checked by the flow

Clocking covers the clock-management tiles (MMCM and PLL), clock buffers and safe switching between clocks. So that an MMCM setting is computed rather than guessed, the flow offers a search over the legal settings for a target frequency, and the design-rule check after synthesis fails any run whose setting is out of range.

Measured. Of 4 probe designs (one legal, three with an MMCM setting outside its range), Vivado's design-rule check after synthesis flagged the setting as out of range on 3. source Two clocks through a BUFGMUX, with paths between one clock's own registers and the mux fanout: setup slack was -6.63 ns unconstrained and 0.419 ns once the source clocks were declared exclusive on xc7a35t (-6.03 and 0.01 ns on xck26), because the clock group also removed the real crossing (Vivado 2026.1, routed). source The clock_mux check on the recorded clock-mux designs (xc7a35t and xck26) blocked 36 and passed 22; every design whose clock groups hid a path was among those blocked. source An RTL clock mux (a LUT) switched 40 times at times unrelated to its clocks gave 40 runt pulses in simulation; Vivado reports it only as TIMING-14. source

Not yet shown. The Clocking Wizard's own settings were compared with the flow's search on only a few target frequencies. The clock-mux check reads a netlist dump made in the check suite's Vivado session; it is not a stage gate, and the CE-select glitch it warns about was not shown in simulation (the unisim model has no timing). No agent run has been graded on a clocking task yet.

Differs by family
  • Bank types and VCCO limits: Artix-7 and the Zynq-7020 have only HR banks (1.2-3.3 V); the K26 has HP banks (1.8 V maximum) and HD banks (up to 3.3 V). tool-checked
  • IDELAY tap resolution and modes: IDELAYE2 has 32 taps of about 78 ps at a 200 MHz reference; IDELAYE3 has 512 taps of 2.1-12 ps and a TIME mode that holds a delay in picoseconds across voltage and temperature. tool-checked
  • SERDES primitives and native mode: ISERDESE2/OSERDESE2 take widths up to 8 (14 cascaded) with BITSLIP; UltraScale+ ISERDESE3/OSERDESE3 take 4 or 8 with no bitslip, and native mode (RX/TX_BITSLICE) is a different design style. tool-checked
  • Differential and termination standards: On HR banks LVDS_25 outputs need VCCO 2.5 V and DIFF_TERM; on UltraScale+ HP banks LVDS runs at 1.8 V with DIFF_TERM_ADV, plus ODT and PRE_EMPHASIS that 7-series lacks. tool-checked
  • Transceivers: Artix-7 has GTP transceivers (none on the Nexys A7 part) and the Zynq-7020 has none; the K26 has GTH transceivers with different wizards and reference-clock buffers. from the guides
  • Logic primitives: 7-series slices have CARRY4 and MUXF7/F8; UltraScale+ CLBs have one CARRY8 and MUXF9, and a 7-series CARRY4 instance has no ZU+ lib cell. tool-checked
  • Ethernet MAC attachment: Artix-7 and the ZU+ PL have no hard Ethernet MAC: a fabric MAC drives a PHY through SelectIO (RMII, MII, RGMII). Both Zynq PS families have hard GEMs: RGMII through MIO, GMII through EMIO to PL logic. from the guides
  • DDR input and output registers (RGMII): RGMII's DDR registers are IDDR/ODDR on 7-series and IDDRE1/ODDRE1 on UltraScale+; both have a same-edge-pipelined input mode, but ODDRE1 has no opposite-edge mode. from the guides
  • LUT RAM shapes for table emulation: Both families have 32-, 64-, 128- and 256-deep single-bit LUT RAMs; UltraScale+ adds RAM32M16, RAM64M8, RAM256X1D and RAM512X1S, so wide multi-port shapes and 512-deep columns exist only there. from the guides
  • GTH/GTY TX phase-interpolator ppm controller (a controlled line-rate offset): UltraScale/UltraScale+ GTH/GTY can program a ppm offset of the TX line rate (244 ppm a step with the Wizard's settings). With one reference the user clocks follow it: a loopback sees none (KR260: 0.0 ppm). from the guides
  • GTH/GTY on-chip RX eye scan (margin analysis) and the attributes the Wizard leaves off: Every GT family has an RX eye-scan block (ES_* attributes, prescale, error and sample counters) measuring margin after the equaliser. The Wizard leaves it off on GTHE4: set ES_EYE_SCAN_EN and ES_ERRDET_EN in the build. from the guides
  • GTH/GTY transmit and receive buffer bypass (fixed, lower transceiver latency): Both families can bypass the TX and RX buffers, cutting the GT delay by tens of UI for a phase-alignment procedure. On the K26 the Wizard builds it from two properties; AR 68177 gives the saving; not board-measured yet. from the guides
  • Registers in the I/O block (IOB packing): A marked first input or last output flop packs into the I/O logic on both families when only the pad is next to it; failure is a critical warning that drc_clean never sees. BELs, messages and DRC rows differ. tool-checked
  • LVCMOS output drive, slew and output resistance: DRIVE is a current limit, not an impedance: 7-series HR LVCMOS33 is 96 ohm at 4 mA and 15 ohm at 16 mA into 50 ohm, 2:1 over corners; only DCI and OUTPUT_IMPEDANCE outputs target 40, 48 or 60 ohm. from the guides

Data movement and DMAMoving blocks of data between memory and a stream, choosing between AMD's DMA cores and a small engine of our own, descriptors, bursts and the page-boundary rule, interrupts, and the software cache contract on the Zynq ports.10 skills, 2 flow checks Knowledge only

A DMA engine moves blocks of data between memory and a stream, or between two memories, without the processor touching each word. The area covers the choice of engine (AMD's AXI DMA, central, multichannel and video DMA, the PCIe bridges, or a small engine written for the job), how descriptors, burst length and the number of bursts in flight set the throughput, the page-boundary rule every burst must keep, how a transfer's end is reported to software, and, on the Zynq processors, whether the caches see what the fabric reads and writes. The agent knows this from skills, asks the standing intake questions when a request moves blocks of data, and has an advisory check that flags a design reaching processor memory through a port that does not snoop the caches without a stated software contract. The library holds one engine of our own, simulated and proved against the AXI rules, and measured in simulation only: no board run exists yet.

Modelled, not measured (the model on AMD's IBIS data and the vendor's board file; no scope, no Vivado run). In simulation against a memory with 20 clocks of read latency, our scatter-gather engine reads 0.2 of the memory port's limit for descriptors of 64 bytes, 0.94 for 4096 bytes and 0.98 for 16,384 bytes, with four bursts in flight; with one burst in flight it stays at 0.44. Descriptor overhead, not the data, sets the small-transfer figure. source 22 of 22 seeded faults in the engine (a burst across a page boundary, a wrong length, a lost beat under back pressure, an ignored error response, a late interrupt and others) were caught by the simulation bench. source 6 of 6 proofs by k-induction passed: the valid-held-until-ready rules on every channel the engine drives, burst size, alignment and the page-boundary rule, at three data widths in both directions; write data after its address and the burst limit are proved to a bounded depth only. source

DSP slices: registers, cascades and speed against latencyWhich registers to turn on around a multiplier, how wide multiplies and dot products are built from slices, and how clock speed trades against latency.9 skills, 1 flow check Measured in Vivado

A DSP slice is a multiplier and an adder with registers built into it. How fast a design that uses one can be clocked depends on which of those registers are turned on, and the agent writes them in the RTL so that synthesis packs them into the slice. Wider multiplies and long sums are built from several slices, either left to the tool or chained by hand, and each choice trades clocks of latency against clock speed and fabric logic. The agent knows this from skills and from measurements on two parts; an advisory check, not a gate, flags a slice with too few registers for the target clock period.

Measured. Best clock speed against the number of registers around one multiply on the Artix-7 part, none to four registers: 175.2, 318.7, 326.6, 464.3 and 464.3 MHz. The step from two to three registers (the output register) is the large one; a fourth adds nothing (the register-count table in the results README; one routed run per point). source The same on the UltraScale+ part: 354.2, 571.4, 678, 725.2 and 725.2 MHz. The three-register figure is the clock-buffer limit of the part (the setup estimate at the target clock is higher); the data sheet's own DSP limit for the part is lower still and is the ceiling at sign-off. source On the Artix-7 part, a multiply of two narrow operands with operand, multiplier and output registers around it reached 464.3 MHz, which is the slice's own limit; with the output register left out it reached 326.6 MHz, and with no register at all 175.2 MHz (one routed run each, Vivado 2026.1). source On the UltraScale+ part the same three cases reached 725.2, 595.9 and 354.2 MHz; with every register the result is limited by the clock buffer (and the clock period asked for), not by the slice. source A single register after the multiply was packed into the slice's multiplier register (MREG 1, output register PREG 0) rather than its output register. With an asynchronous reset on that register it was packed into the output register instead, the multiplier stayed unregistered, and the slice ran at 198.9 MHz instead of 318.7 with a synchronous reset (Artix-7). AMD's methodology guide (UG949) describes this packing: the slice registers have synchronous resets only, so a register with an asynchronous reset is built in the fabric. source A signed multiply of two thirty-two-bit operands on the Artix-7 part, with the same registers at the ends: the tool's own split used 4 slices and reached 251.6 MHz; a hand-written cascade of slices (with the seventeen-bit shift) used 4 and reached 306.3 MHz; partial products summed in the fabric used 4 and reached 194.3 MHz. source An eight-term dot product on the Artix-7 part: a chain of slices (cascade) reached 464.3 MHz at 10 register stages; the same sum with a fabric adder tree, a register after every level, reached 328.4 MHz at 6 stages, so the tree saves clocks and loses speed. source The same two on the UltraScale+ part: the cascade reached 656.2 MHz at 10 stages (the delay registers that skew its operands became shift-register cells that limit the clock) and the tree 702.2 MHz at 6 stages. source A pre-adder before the multiplier was used by the slice and ran at 464.3 MHz with its own register and 290.4 MHz without it. A constant compare on an accumulator used the slice's pattern detector (PATDET); four twelve-bit adds written for the SIMD mode were not mapped to a slice (0 slices) with this coding. source

The coverage check is an evidence file that the flow can add to the second verification stage; it is off unless asked for.

Signal integrity at the pinsChoosing the output drive, slew and series resistor of an off-chip link so the receiver stays inside the data sheet limits, for the board as built and for a board whose resistor can still change.2 skills, 2 flow checks Knowledge only

A signal that leaves the chip runs down a board trace, and the trace can send the edge back as overshoot and ringing at the receiver. The agent models one output at the pins: it takes the driver tables that AMD publishes for each I/O standard, the line's length and impedance and the receiver's capacitance, and finds out whether the receiver stays inside the data sheet's overshoot and undershoot limits, whether its first wave is large enough and whether the edge rings back through the threshold. It then proposes the drive strength, slew rate and series resistor. A finished board cannot always change its resistor, so it gives two answers for each port: the best setting for the board as built, and the best setup if the resistor can change. The check is advisory, nothing in the flow stops a run that disagrees with it, and every figure here is a model result, not a scope measurement.

Modelled, not measured (the model on AMD's IBIS data and the vendor's board file; no scope, no Vivado run). A sweep of 868 model runs (DRIVE, SLEW, series resistor and line length, three IBIS corners in each) found that 22 ohm in series on a 6 in line passes 0 of 8 DRIVE and SLEW combinations for LVCMOS33 in 7-series HR banks, against 6 of 8 with 47 ohm, and 8 of 15 for LVCMOS18 in UltraScale+ HP banks. source With the KR260 carrier's measured routing (port A 7.2 in, port B 2.8 in, no series resistor on either), the transmit pins as the design sets them (DRIVE 12 mA FAST) put the receiver 1.06 V beyond the rail on port A and 1 V on port B, against the 0.2 V limit; the model puts that right with 27 ohm in series on port A and 22 ohm on port B. source With no resistor on the board, no setting meets every limit on port A: the best, DRIVE 4 mA, keeps the overshoot inside the limit but its receiver edge takes 2.08 ns at the weak corner against the 1 ns the house rule allows. If a resistor can be fitted, 7 combinations pass, and the check proposes 27 ohm. source A second solver, ngspice, run on 24 cases with a series resistor, agreed with the model's receiver waveform to within 0.04 V. source On 12 falling edges with nothing in series the two solvers disagree by up to 0.59 V; the cause is not found. source Routed lengths read from the vendor's KR260 board file: the 15 length-matched RGMII nets of port A run 7.07 to 7.34 in on the carrier. source The 14 length-matched RGMII nets of port B run 2.34 to 2.87 in. source
