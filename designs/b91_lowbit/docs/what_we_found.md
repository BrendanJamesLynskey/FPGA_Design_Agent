# What we found: low-bit number formats for nanosecond inference (brief 91)

An engineer's account. Every number below is from a committed file: `results/b91/accuracy.json`,
`accuracy_seedmean.json`, `summary.json` (the hardware rows, from `results/b91/ooc/`), `rounds/` and `frozen.json`; the
reference points are from `results/b87/` and `results/b89/`. The tables are generated by `results/b91/tables.py`. The
data are brief 87's synthetic order flow (3 classes, label = sign of the mid move 40 events ahead). The model
definitions are in [design.md](design.md); the rule that chose the networks was committed before any network was
trained or scored ([preregistration.md](preregistration.md)).

## 1. The short answer

- **Accuracy does not depend much on the format, as long as a weight has at least about 16 levels.** INT4, MXFP4-style,
  our power-of-two format (`log4`, with or without a zero) and our two-term format all land at 0.484-0.490 test accuracy
  (mean of three seeds), which is level with the 87 boosted trees (0.4914) and with the 89 `lg_small` network (0.4892);
  their paired differences to `lg_small` all include zero (post-hoc table below). Ternary and binary lose about 0.02.
- **None of the formats is on the accuracy-against-LUTs or accuracy-against-latency front.** The fronts are made of
  the 87 and 89 points (the trivial rule, the 87 trees, the 89 `lg_b87` and `lg_small` networks, TreeLUT and the 87
  MLP). The 4-bit-weight nets that match `lg_small`'s accuracy need 1,406 to 2,068 LUTs and 10.05 to 11.25 ns combinational
  on the K26 (and 1,241 to 1,673 LUTs registered per layer); `lg_small` reaches the same accuracy in 325 LUTs and 4.5 ns.
- **So: a fixed number format does not beat a network of arbitrary small learned functions here**, and not by a wide
  margin: about 4 to 6 times the LUTs and 2.2 to 2.5 times the latency for the same accuracy (`log4_nz`: 1,406 LUTs and 11.0 ns against 325 and 4.5), at the sizes we tried.
- **Within the formats, the power-of-two format is the cheapest at equal accuracy:** `log4` uses 1,591 LUTs against
  INT4's 2,068 and MXFP4's 2,056 on the K26 (same shape and activation width), and `log4_nz` 1,406. A shift is free,
  and the saving is the adders that INT4 and MXFP4 need for the elements with two terms. The two-term `log4p1` is not
  better than the single-term form on this data (it reached 0.4845 with a smaller network).
- **A wider activation is a loss on both axes.** 8-bit activations cost 1.5 times the LUTs of 4-bit and were not more
  accurate; 2-bit activations lost about 0.02.
- **Nothing here needs a DSP or a block RAM:** every build reports 0 DSP and 0 BRAM.

## 2. What we built

`designs/b91_lowbit/lowbit/`: a trainer with the quantisers in the loop for eight formats, an exact integer twin, an
RTL emitter and a driver; `flow/lowbit.py` is the flow-tool wrapper. A weight is `c x e`, with `e` an element of the
format's grid and `c` a power of two shared by the neuron, so that the scale becomes the neuron's requantisation shift.
A neuron is a balanced tree of adds and subtracts of shifted inputs plus a bias, then a shift and a clip; there is no
multiplier. [generator.md](generator.md) is the datasheet.

| Format | Element grid (integer units) | Bits |
|---|---|---|
| `binary_t3`, `binary_t7` | +-1 on thermometer inputs (3 or 7 thresholds per feature) | 1 |
| `ternary` | -1, 0, +1 | 2 |
| `int4` | -7 .. +7 | 4 |
| `mxfp4` | E2M1 magnitudes x 2: 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12 | 4 |
| `log4` | 0, powers of two 1 .. 64 | 4 |
| `log4_nz` | powers of two 1 .. 128, no zero | 4 |
| `log4p1` | 0, then 2^e (1 or 1.5) x 2: 2, 3, 4, 6 .. 192, 256 | 5 |

Two shapes (hidden 16-8 and hidden 8), three seeds, 4-bit activations (stage 1); the two best non-binary families by
validation (`log4` and `mxfp4`) were swept over 2-bit and 8-bit activations (stage 2). The rule and its outcome are in
`results/b91/rounds/` (`stage1.json`, `select.json`, `stage2.json`) and `frozen.json`. The shapes the rule chose differ
between formats (hidden 8 for `binary_t3`, `ternary` and `log4p1`; 16-8 for the rest), so a comparison across formats
compares format and size together; the stage 1 table gives both shapes for every format.

## 4. Reading the numbers

**Accuracy.** Every network beats the trivial rule: the paired intervals against it are above zero, with
differences of +0.0278 (`binary_t7`) to +0.0652 (`log4_nz`) for the built seeds. The three-seed spread is as large as
most differences between formats (for example `log4_nz` scores 0.4919, 0.4847 and 0.4807 on its three seeds), so the
built-seed intervals in the first table understate the uncertainty; the post-hoc seed-mean table is the one to quote
for ranking. In it, INT4, MXFP4, `log4`, `log4_nz` and `log4p1` are each within 0.005 of `lg_small` with an interval
containing zero, and `mxfp4` at A = 4 is nominally above it (+0.0005, interval [-0.0054, +0.0063]). We do not claim any
of the 4-bit-weight formats is better than another. The selected networks' ranking on validation (all within 0.0013) is
not reproduced on test, which is what a ceiling of about 0.48 on validation predicts.

**The stage 2 rule picked `mxfp4` at A = 8** by a validation margin of 0.0001 over A = 4 (0.4852 against 0.4851). On the
test split A = 8 is worse (0.4840 against 0.4897 as a seed mean) and 1.5 times larger. The pre-registered rule stands,
and the A = 4 network is reported beside it.

**Where the area goes.** The 16-8 nets have 264 weights, but each neuron sums seven, sixteen or eight shifted 4-bit codes
into an accumulator sized for the worst case, and 27 of them make adder trees of 1,400 to 2,100 LUTs for the
combinational form, where the 89 `lg_small` network, 27 small tables, fits in 325. The format changes the number of terms (INT4 and MXFP4 need up to two per weight, `log4` exactly one,
ternary and binary zero or one), so it matters; but the dominant cost is the adder trees, not the multiplications. That
is the real reason the 89 LUT networks win: a table lookup replaces an adder tree.

**Latency.** The combinational form is faster than the per-layer registered form for every network (for example
`log4` 10.2 against 13.0 ns, `binary_t3` 3.3 against 6.15 ns), because the registered form closes at a shorter period
(2.05 to 4.0 ns) but pays three or four clocks, and its period is floored at 2.0 ns when quoted. A deeper pipeline
would only pay with a faster clock target than these nets reach.

**The Artix-7 100T** closes the same networks about 2.7-3.0 times slower than the K26 (for example `binary_t3` comb 9.8
against 3.3 ns, `log4p1` comb 19.8 against 6.85 ns), with a similar or smaller LUT count (INT4 comb: 1,697 against 2,068), as the 89 networks did. `mxfp4_h16x8_a4_comb` did
not close on the Artix-7 in six passes (three, then three more) and is marked "not built" there.

## 5. Dead ends and corrections

- **Binary, first attempt.** The first binary run collapsed (it did not beat the majority class on validation in a
  short trial; that trial was not recorded). Two causes, both fixed before the frozen run: the initial latent weights
  were set to +-1, which sits at the clip so that a learning rate of 3e-3 could not flip a sign, and the integer scores
  were used as logits without a scale. Initial latent weights are now small and random (the sign is the weight), and the
  loss scale starts from the standard deviation of the scores (the brief 89 lesson again).
- **Selecting by tie rules.** The shape rule picks the smaller network on ties, and the stage 2 rule picks the smaller A;
  both were fixed in the pre-registration. The `mxfp4` A = 8 choice shows the cost of a rule that is correct but
  decided by a 0.0001 margin.
- **Not tried:** a real-valued per-neuron scale (the power-of-two restriction costs an unmeasured amount of accuracy),
  log-domain rounding, per-format tuning of the learning rate, learned sparsity (many weights are small; ternary shows
  half can be zero), narrower accumulators, and using the formats inside the LUT networks' neurons.

## 6. What this means

- For networks this small (264 weights, 7 features), **do not spend effort on an exotic weight format**: the format has
  little effect on accuracy once there are about 16 levels, and the adder trees dominate the area. If a format must be
  chosen, a power of two is the cheapest.
- The 89 LUT-network approach beat all of these on area and latency at equal accuracy. Weight formats start to matter
  where weights are data, not wiring, which is the phase-2 case ([stage2_plan.md](stage2_plan.md)); phase 1 says nothing
  about it.
- Next steps: (1) a LUT-network neuron whose table is the quantised neuron (hybrid), to keep the table's area advantage
  with INT4-like inputs; (2) ternary and `log4` with trained sparsity and a narrower accumulator; (3) a real-valued
  scale as an ablation to price the power-of-two restriction; (4) the stage 2 plan.

## 7. How to reproduce

```
python -m designs.b91_lowbit.lowbit.cli stage1 && ... select && ... stage2 && ... freeze && ... rtl
B91_RECORD=results/b91 pytest designs/b91_lowbit/tests flow/tests/test_91_lowbit.py
python -m designs.b91_lowbit.lowbit.evaluate && python -m designs.b91_lowbit.lowbit.seedmean
python -m designs.b91_lowbit.ooc.ooc --part xck26-sfvc784-2LV-c --arms <modules>     # Vivado 2026.1, a heavy slot
python3 results/b91/summary.py && python3 results/b91/tables.py
```
